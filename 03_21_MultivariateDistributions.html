21  Multivariate Distributions

Most datasets record more than two variables for each observation. A worker has a wage, years of schooling, years of experience, and a sex.

This chapter asks three questions.

  • How do we describe three or more variables before fitting a model?
    • Three-way tables: these extend the joint distribution to a third discrete variable, \(\hat{p}(x,y,z)\).
    • Scatterplot matrices: these draw the scatterplot for every pair of cardinal variables in one grid.
  • What happens when we hold a third variable fixed?
    • Conditional proportions: these compare groups within one value of a third discrete variable, \(\hat{p}(y \mid x, z)\).
    • Conditioning plots: these draw one scatterplot per group, so any pattern that appears is a within-group pattern.
    • Grouped boxplots: these place the boxplots for one factor side by side within each level of another.
  • When does pooling groups mislead us?
    • Simpson’s paradox: this is when a relationship that holds inside all or most subgroups reverses once the data are pooled.

The recurring lesson is that a comparison between two variables can look different once a third variable is held fixed. The only prerequisites are the data frames, proportions, ECDFs, and boxplots from Data & Visualization, and the scatterplot from Bivariate Distributions.

21.1 Discrete Data

Three-Way Tables

Joint Distributions tabulated two discrete variables at once. With three, the table gains a third dimension.

ImportantKey Definition

The joint distribution of three discrete variables \(\hat{x}_{i}\), \(\hat{y}_{i}\), and \(\hat{z}_{i}\) is the share of observations with each combination of values: \[\hat{p}(x,y,z) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\left( \hat{x}_{i}=x, \hat{y}_{i}=y, \hat{z}_{i}=z \right)/n.\] Summing over the values of one variable collapses the table to the two-way joint distribution of the other two, for example \(\hat{p}(x,y) = \sum_{z} \hat{p}(x,y,z)\).

A three-way table is useful whenever a two-way comparison might depend on a third factor. It keeps every combination visible. Collapsing it recovers the two-way table you would have made had you ignored the third variable. R can store counts directly as a three-way table, such as the built-in UCBAdmissions.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# UCBAdmissions counts graduate applications to six Berkeley departments in 1973.
# Three factors: Admit (2 levels), Gender (2 levels), Dept (6 levels)
dim(UCBAdmissions)
## [1] 2 2 6
dimnames(UCBAdmissions)
## $Admit
## [1] "Admitted" "Rejected"
## 
## $Gender
## [1] "Male"   "Female"
## 
## $Dept
## [1] "A" "B" "C" "D" "E" "F"

# ftable() flattens the table so it prints on one page
ftable(UCBAdmissions, row.vars=c('Dept', 'Gender'), col.vars='Admit')
##             Admit Admitted Rejected
## Dept Gender                        
## A    Male              512      313
##      Female             89       19
## B    Male              353      207
##      Female             17        8
## C    Male              120      205
##      Female            202      391
## D    Male              138      279
##      Female            131      244
## E    Male               53      138
##      Female             94      299
## F    Male               22      351
##      Female             24      317

Dividing by the total number of applicants gives the joint distribution, and margin.table() collapses it.

Code 
# Joint distribution over all 24 cells
n <- sum(UCBAdmissions)
p_xyz <- UCBAdmissions / n

# Collapse over Dept to get the two-way distribution of Admit and Gender
p_xy <- margin.table(p_xyz, c(1, 2))
round(p_xy, 3)
##           Gender
## Admit       Male Female
##   Admitted 0.265  0.123
##   Rejected 0.330  0.282

# Collapse further to the marginal distribution of Gender
round(margin.table(p_xyz, 2), 3)
## Gender
##   Male Female 
##  0.595  0.405
NoteMust Know

Suppose \(n=20\) workers are recorded by sex (\(x\)), whether they were promoted (\(y\)), and whether they hold a degree (\(z\)).

Workers by Sex, Promotion, and Degree
No degree Degree
Men, promoted 2 4
Men, not promoted 6 2
Women, promoted 1 3
Women, not promoted 1 1

The joint proportion of promoted men without a degree is \(\hat{p}(\text{man}, \text{promoted}, \text{no degree}) = 2/20 = 0.10\). Collapsing over degree, the share of promoted men is \(\hat{p}(\text{man}, \text{promoted}) = (2+4)/20 = 0.30\). Collapsing again, the marginal share of men is \((2+6+4+2)/20 = 0.70\).

Code 
# Build the same table from a small data frame, one row per worker
xy0_hat <- data.frame(
    sex=rep(c('man', 'woman'), c(14, 6)),
    degree=c(rep(c('no', 'yes'), c(8, 6)), rep(c('no', 'yes'), c(2, 4))),
    promoted=c(rep(c(1, 0), c(2, 6)), rep(c(1, 0), c(4, 2)),
        rep(c(1, 0), c(1, 1)), rep(c(1, 0), c(3, 1))))
tab <- table(xy0_hat)
ftable(tab, row.vars=c('sex', 'promoted'), col.vars='degree')
##                degree no yes
## sex   promoted              
## man   0                6   2
##       1                2   4
## woman 0                1   1
##       1                1   3

# Collapse over degree
round(margin.table(tab / sum(tab), c(1, 3)), 2)
##        promoted
## sex       0   1
##   man   0.4 0.3
##   woman 0.1 0.2

Conditional Proportions

Collapsing hides the third variable. Conditioning does the opposite: it fixes the third variable at one value and compares within that slice.

ImportantKey Definition

The conditional distribution of \(\hat{y}_{i}\) given \(\hat{x}_{i}=x\) and \(\hat{z}_{i}=z\) is the share of observations with \(\hat{y}_{i}=y\) inside the subgroup where both conditioning variables take the stated values: \[\hat{p}(y \mid x, z) = \frac{\hat{p}(x,y,z)}{\hat{p}(x,z)}.\]

Conditional proportions are useful for comparing groups on equal footing. For example, the admission rate for women in department A is compared with the rate for men in department A. So differences in which departments people apply to cannot drive the comparison. In R, prop.table() with a margin= argument divides each cell by the total of its slice.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# Admission rate by Gender, pooling all departments
p_pooled <- prop.table(margin.table(UCBAdmissions, c(1, 2)), margin=2)
round(p_pooled['Admitted', ], 2)
##   Male Female 
##   0.45   0.30
# Pooled over departments, men were admitted at a higher rate than women.

# Admission rate by Gender within each Dept
p_within <- prop.table(UCBAdmissions, margin=c(2, 3))
round(p_within['Admitted', , ], 2)
##         Dept
## Gender      A    B    C    D    E    F
##   Male   0.62 0.63 0.37 0.33 0.28 0.06
##   Female 0.82 0.68 0.34 0.35 0.24 0.07
# Women had higher rates than men in four of the six: A, B, D, and F.
# The gaps in D and F are small.
# Men had higher rates in C and E, so the reversal holds in most departments, not all.

The pooled and within-group tables are both correct summaries of the same data. They can differ when the slices differ in their rates and in their composition, which the pooled table mixes together. The code below computes both.

Code 
# Where did each gender apply? Share of each gender's applications going to each Dept
p_apply <- prop.table(margin.table(UCBAdmissions, c(2, 3)), margin=1)
round(p_apply, 2)
##         Dept
## Gender      A    B    C    D    E    F
##   Male   0.31 0.21 0.12 0.15 0.07 0.14
##   Female 0.06 0.01 0.32 0.20 0.21 0.19
# Just over half of the men applied to departments A and B.
# Over 90% of the women applied to departments C to F.

# How selective is each Dept? Admission rate by Dept, pooling genders
p_dept <- prop.table(margin.table(UCBAdmissions, c(1, 3)), margin=2)
round(p_dept['Admitted', ], 2)
##    A    B    C    D    E    F 
## 0.64 0.63 0.35 0.34 0.25 0.06
# Departments A and B admitted over 60% of their applicants.
# Departments C to F admitted about 35% of their applicants or fewer.
# The pooled gap mostly reflects where people applied, not how they were treated.

The descriptive lesson is to report the within-group comparison alongside the pooled one, and say how the groups differ in composition.

21.2 Continuous Data

Scatterplot Matrices

For two cardinal variables, the basic figure is the scatterplot: one point per observation, with \(\hat{x}_{i}\) on the horizontal axis and \(\hat{y}_{i}\) on the vertical axis. With several cardinal variables, there is one scatterplot for each pair. A scatterplot matrix draws all of them in one grid, so you can scan every pairwise relationship at once.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# Store the observed data, one row per worker
# The data cover 3294 US workers.
# wage is the hourly wage in 1980 dollars, and school and exper are in years.
data(Wages1, package='Ecdat')
xy_hat <- Wages1

# Every pair of the three cardinal variables, one panel each
pairs(xy_hat[, c('wage', 'school', 'exper')], pch=16, col=grey(0, .1), main=NA)
Code 
# Row wage, column school plots wage (vertical) against schooling (horizontal).
# Schooling and experience are whole numbers, so their panels form stripes.
# More schooling goes with higher wages and with less experience.

Each panel plots the variable named in its row on the vertical axis against the variable named in its column on the horizontal axis. The panel below the diagonal repeats the panel above it with the axes swapped, so you only need to read one triangle. The psych package adds histograms on the diagonal and a summary of each pairwise relationship in the upper triangle.

Code 
# pairs.panels lays out univariate histograms on the diagonal,
# scatterplots in the lower triangle, and correlations in the upper triangle.
# With this many workers, smoother=TRUE shades each scatterplot by point density.
psych::pairs.panels(xy_hat[, c('wage', 'school', 'exper')],
    hist.col=grey(0, .25), breaks=30, density=FALSE, hist.border=NA, # Diagonal
    ellipses=FALSE, rug=FALSE, smoother=FALSE, pch=16, col=rgb(0, .7, .7, .8) # Lower Triangle
    )
Code 
# Wages have a long right tail, and schooling piles up at 12 years.
TipTest Yourself

The diagonal shows univariate histograms. The lower triangle shows scatterplots between each pair of variables, shaded darker where observations pile up. Encoding More Variables

A scatterplot has two axes, but a point has more attributes than position: color, shape, and size can each carry another variable. With many observations the points overlap, so differences in size are hard to see. The figure below therefore draws one point for each combination of the horizontal variable and a factor, placed at the mean of the vertical variable. Color and shape mark the factor, and size marks how many observations the point summarizes.

Code 
# One row per combination of schooling and sex: mean wage and number of workers
xy_cell_hat <- aggregate(wage ~ school + sex, data=xy_hat, FUN=mean)
xy_cell_hat$n_cell <- aggregate(wage ~ school + sex, data=xy_hat, FUN=length)$wage

# Color and shape mark sex
cols <- c(rgb(.8, 0, 0, .5), rgb(0, 0, .8, .5))
pchs <- c(16, 17)

# Point area scales with the number of workers
sizes <- sqrt(xy_cell_hat$n_cell / max(xy_cell_hat$n_cell)) * 3

# Legends go in the margin above the plot so they cover no points
par(mar=c(4, 4, 4, 1))
plot(wage ~ school, data=xy_cell_hat,
    pch=pchs[xy_cell_hat$sex], col=cols[xy_cell_hat$sex], cex=sizes,
    xlab='Years of schooling',
    ylab='Mean hourly wage (1980 dollars)', main=NA)
legend('topleft', inset=c(0, -.14), xpd=TRUE, horiz=TRUE, bty='n',
    legend=levels(xy_cell_hat$sex),
    pch=pchs, col=cols)
legend('topright', inset=c(0, -.14), xpd=TRUE, horiz=TRUE, bty='n',
    legend=c('10 workers', '500 workers'),
    pch=16, col=grey(0, .5), pt.cex=sqrt(c(10, 500) / max(xy_cell_hat$n_cell)) * 3)
Code 
# Men's mean wage is above women's at all 12 schooling levels where both appear.
# Both means rise with schooling from 9 to 15 years.
# The largest points are at 12 years, with 594 workers of each sex.
# The smallest points hold only a few workers, so their means jump around.

Extra channels can show patterns that the plain panel of the matrix hid. Each added channel makes the figure harder to read, so two extra variables is a practical limit. Interactive versions, where hovering over a point reveals every variable at once, are covered in Communicating Results.

21.3 Mixed Data

Conditioning Plots

A scatterplot can also be split by a factor: draw it separately for each group. A conditioning plot holds the factor fixed within each panel, so any pattern that appears is a within-group pattern. Comparing Multiple Groups summarizes one factor at a time with boxplots and ECDFs; here we go straight to conditioning, and then combine two factors at once below.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# Three-level schooling factor, as in Comparing Multiple Groups
xy_hat$school_g <- cut(xy_hat$school, breaks=c(0, 11, 12, 16), labels=c('<12', '12', '>12'))

The line in each panel joins the group’s mean outcome across values of the horizontal variable, so the eye can follow the center of the cloud.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# One scatterplot per schooling group, on common axes to compare the panels
# Experience is on the horizontal axis, since the groups already use schooling.
# The wage axis ends just above 20 dollars, leaving out the 16 highest wages.
par(mfrow=c(1, 3))
for (g in levels(xy_hat$school_g)) {
    xy_g_hat <- xy_hat[xy_hat$school_g == g, ]
    plot(wage ~ exper, data=xy_g_hat, pch=16, col=grey(0, .1),
        xlim=range(xy_hat$exper), ylim=c(0, 20),
        xlab='Years of experience', ylab='Hourly wage (1980 dollars)', main=NA)
    title(paste0(g, ' years of schooling'), font.main=1)
    # Mean wage at each experience value held by 20 or more workers
    m_g_hat <- aggregate(wage ~ exper, data=xy_g_hat, FUN=mean)
    n_g <- table(xy_g_hat$exper)
    lines(wage ~ exper, data=m_g_hat[n_g >= 20, ], col=rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lwd=2)
}
Code 
# Pooled, wage and experience are nearly unrelated (correlation 0.05).
# Under 12 years: the mean wage stays near 5 dollars after 5 years of experience.
# Exactly 12 years: the mean wage rises from about 5 to about 6 dollars.
# Over 12 years: the mean wage is the highest at every experience value drawn.
# Its line also rises up to 9 years, then falls where few workers remain.

A pooled figure can hide within-group patterns like these when the groups differ in their averages.

The pooled and within-group views can be placed on one figure. We mark each group with its own symbol and add each group’s center as a large hollow symbol. The hollow marker does not cover the observations beneath it.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# Small filled points: workers, by schooling; large hollow points: group means
# The wage axis ends just above 20 dollars, leaving out the 16 highest wages.
g_hues <- c(rgb(.8, 0, 0), rgb(0, .8, 0), rgb(0, 0, .8))
g_cols <- adjustcolor(g_hues, alpha.f=.1)
g_pch <- c(15, 16, 17)
g_pch_hollow <- c(0, 1, 2)
par(mar=c(4, 4, 4, 1))
plot(wage ~ exper, data=xy_hat, pch=g_pch[xy_hat$school_g], col=g_cols[xy_hat$school_g],
    ylim=c(0, 20),
    xlab='Years of experience', ylab='Hourly wage (1980 dollars)', main=NA)
means <- aggregate(cbind(exper, wage) ~ school_g, data=xy_hat, FUN=mean)
points(means$exper, means$wage, pch=g_pch_hollow, col=g_hues, cex=2.5, lwd=2)

# Legends in the margin above the plot
legend('topleft', inset=c(0, -.14), xpd=TRUE, horiz=TRUE, bty='n',
    legend=paste0(levels(xy_hat$school_g), ' years'), pch=g_pch, col=g_hues)
legend('topright', inset=c(0, -.14), xpd=TRUE, bty='n',
    legend='group mean', pch=1, col='black', pt.cex=1.8, pt.lwd=2)
Code 
# The most-schooled group has the highest mean wage and the least experience.
# So the pooled cloud looks flatter than the panels with 12 or more years.
# Every worker with over 12 years of experience has under 12 years of schooling.

NoteMust Know

Conditional means can reverse when pooled, for the same compositional reason as conditional proportions. Suppose \(40\) men and \(40\) women work at a firm, split by whether they hold a degree, with the mean hourly wage in each cell shown below.

Mean Hourly Wage by Sex and Degree
Men Women
No degree \(n=10\), mean \(10\) \(n=30\), mean \(11\)
Degree \(n=30\), mean \(20\) \(n=10\), mean \(21\)

Within each degree group, women earn \(1\) more per hour. Pooled, men earn \((10 \cdot 10 + 30 \cdot 20)/40 = 17.5\) and women earn \((30 \cdot 11 + 10 \cdot 21)/40 = 13.5\), so women earn \(4\) less. The pooled mean is a weighted average of the within-group means, \(\hat{m}_{Y}(x) = \sum_{z} \hat{m}_{Y}(x, z)\, \hat{p}(z \mid x)\). The weights differ by sex, since three quarters of the women and only one quarter of the men are in the no-degree group.

Code 
# Rebuild the pooled means from the cell means and cell sizes
m_men_hat <- (10 * 10 + 30 * 20) / (10 + 30)
m_women_hat <- (30 * 11 + 10 * 21) / (30 + 10)
c(men=m_men_hat, women=m_women_hat)
##   men women 
##  17.5  13.5

Two Factors at Once

A group summary is not limited to one factor. Crossing two factors gives one group for each combination of levels, each smaller than the groups of either factor alone but still worth comparing.

Code 
# Schooling group (3 levels) crossed with sex (2 levels) gives six groups
table(xy_hat$school_g, xy_hat$sex)
##      
##       female male
##   <12    567  786
##   12     594  594
##   >12    408  345
# Even the smallest group holds 345 workers.

A grouped boxplot places the boxes for one factor side by side within each level of the other, so a within-group comparison and a between-group comparison are both visible at once. Color keeps the group hues used in Conditioning Plots above, and a lighter or darker shade of each hue marks the second factor, so hue and shade carry two different variables.

Code 
## MAIN EXAMPLE
# Hue stays fixed to each schooling group; shade (light/dark) marks sex.
# Groups keep their own order, which also runs from low to high median wage.
col_f_shade <- adjustcolor(g_hues, alpha.f=.35)
col_m_shade <- adjustcolor(g_hues, alpha.f=.85)
box_cols <- as.vector(rbind(col_f_shade, col_m_shade))
med_cols <- adjustcolor(box_cols, alpha.f=2, red.f=.5, green.f=.5, blue.f=.5) # darker shades, for the medians

# The wage axis ends just above 20 dollars, leaving out the 16 highest wages.
par(mar=c(4, 4, 4, 1))
boxplot(wage ~ sex + school_g, data=xy_hat,
    col=box_cols, border=NA, medcol=med_cols, xaxt='n', ylim=c(0, 20),
    whisklty=0, staplelty=0, outline=FALSE,
    xlab='Years of schooling', ylab='Hourly wage (1980 dollars)', main=NA)
# Raw Observations, in place of whiskers and outliers
stripchart(wage ~ sex + school_g, data=xy_hat,
    pch='-', col=grey(0, .2), cex=1,
    vert=TRUE, add=TRUE)

21.4 Simpson’s Paradox

A relationship visible inside subgroups can flip direction once those subgroups are pooled. The conditional proportions above compare outcomes within values of a third variable \(\hat{z}_{i}\), so we can compare \(\hat{p}(y\mid x,z)\) within each group to the pooled \(\hat{p}(y\mid x)\).

ImportantKey Definition

Simpson’s paradox (Yule 1903; Simpson 1951) arises when a relationship that holds inside all or most subgroups reverses when the data are pooled. The within-group conditional distributions \(\hat{p}(y\mid x, z)\) point one way; the overall \(\hat{p}(y\mid x)\) points the other.

Simpson’s paradox is useful as a warning whenever you are tempted to pool subgroups: the pooled relationship reflects both the within-group pattern and how the subgroups differ in size. The base-rate fallacy is a related composition effect: a category’s share of a pooled statistic can be driven by its size rather than its rate. The Berkeley admissions in Conditional Proportions are the classic example of the paradox. The example is part of a real debate about discrimination (http://homepage.stat.uiowa.edu/~mbognar/1030/Bickel-Berkeley.pdf). A pooled gap can reflect how groups spread across subgroups rather than differences within those subgroups. The same logic applies to the gender pay gap, cross-country growth comparisons, and many other social issues.1 Neither the pooled rates nor the within-group rates alone identify what would happen under an intervention.

NoteMust Know

A stylized version of the admissions example makes the arithmetic easy to follow. Suppose \(400\) men and \(400\) women apply to one of two departments (English or Engineering). Women can have higher admission rates within both departments yet a lower overall admission rate, if women disproportionately apply to the more selective department (English) and men to the less selective one (Engineering).
