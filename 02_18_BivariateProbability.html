18  Bivariate Theory

The previous chapters summarized bivariate data that we observed. This chapter develops the theoretical counterpart: the bivariate random vector and its joint, marginal, and conditional distributions. Bayes’ theorem then reverses the direction of conditioning. Just as a single random variable has a theoretical mean and variance, a pair \((X_{i}, Y_{i})\) has a theoretical covariance, correlation, and regression coefficient. We then cover the two kinds of testing error and how Bayes’ theorem turns a test result into an updated belief.

18.1 Theoretical Distributions

To talk about what sample statistics estimate, we need a theoretical counterpart to the bivariate observations of the previous chapters.

ImportantKey Definition

A random vector \((X_{i}, Y_{i})\) is a pair of random variables drawn together. It is the population analog of one paired observation \((\hat{x}_{i}, \hat{y}_{i})\), described before the data exist.

The random vector is useful as the formal object that the empirical pair \((\hat{x}_{i}, \hat{y}_{i})\) samples from. For example, if we are going to flip two coins, then \((X_{i}, Y_{i})\) corresponds to the unflipped coins and \((\hat{x}_{i}, \hat{y}_{i})\) to the concrete outcomes once they are flipped. Each sample-level object from earlier chapters (\(\hat{p}(x,y)\), \(\hat{c}_{XY}\), \(\hat{r}_{XY}\), \(\hat{b}_{1}\)) has a population counterpart defined at the level of the random vector.

Definitions for Discrete Data

For discrete random variables, the starting point is the table of probabilities for every possible pair of values.

ImportantKey Definition

The joint probability of two discrete random variables is the probability that both events \(X_{i}=x\) and \(Y_{i}=y\) occur: \[\text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y).\] The joint distribution is the table of these probabilities over all pairs \((x, y)\), summing to one.

The joint probability is useful as the theoretical version of the empirical joint distribution \(\hat{p}(x,y)\) from Bivariate Distributions: the same table structure, but with population probabilities in place of sample proportions. With enough draws, \(\hat{p}(x,y)\) approaches the joint distribution.

A particularly simple special case is when the two variables carry no information about each other.

ImportantKey Definition

Two random variables are statistically independent when the joint probability factors as the product of the marginals: \[\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x, Y_{i}=y)=\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x)\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=y) \quad \text{for every } (x, y).\] Equivalently, knowing \(Y_{i}\) tells you nothing about the distribution of \(X_{i}\).

Independence is useful as the cleanest baseline against which to compare real data: most of the dependence statistics in Statistics of Association take value zero when the variables are independent. Independence is sometimes assumed for mathematical simplicity, not because it generally fits data well.1

The marginal distributions are then defined as \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x) &= \sum_{y} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x | Y_{i} = y) \text{Prob}( Y_{i} = y ) \\ \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = y) &= \sum_{x} \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = y | X_{i} = x) \text{Prob}( X_{i} = x ), \end{aligned}\] which is also known as the law of total probability.

The conditional distributions are defined as \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x | Y_{i} = y) &= \frac{ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y)}{ \text{Prob}( Y_{i} = y )} \\ \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = y | X_{i} = x) &= \frac{ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y)}{ \text{Prob}( X_{i} = x )} \end{aligned}\]

Coin Flips Example

For one example, consider flipping two coins, where we mark whether “heads” is face up with a \(1\) and “tail” with a \(0\). E.g., the first coin has a value of \(x=1\) if it shows heads and \(x=0\) if it shows tails. This table shows both the joint distribution and also each marginal distribution.

\(y=1\) \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=0,Y_{i}=1)\) \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=1,Y_{i}=1)\) \(\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1)\)

NoteMust Know

Suppose both coins are “fair”: \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=1)= 1/2\) and \(\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1|X_{i}=x)=1/2\) for either \(x=1\) or \(x=0\), then the four potential outcomes have equal probabilities. \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0, Y_{i} = 0) &= 1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0, Y_{i} = 1) &= 1/4 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1, Y_{i} = 0) &= 1/4 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1, Y_{i} = 1) &= 1/4 . \end{aligned}\] The joint distribution is written generally as \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y) &= \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x) \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = y). \end{aligned}\]

The marginal distribution of the second coin is \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 0) &= \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 0 | X_{i} = 0) \text{Prob}(X_{i}=0) + \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 0 | X_{i} = 1) \text{Prob}(X_{i}=1) \\ &= 1/2 (1/2) + 1/2 (1/2) = 1/2 \\ \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 1) &= \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 1 | X_{i} = 0) \text{Prob}(X_{i}=0) + \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 1 | X_{i} = 1) \text{Prob}(X_{i}=1) \\ &= 1/2 (1/2) + 1/2 (1/2) = 1/2 \end{aligned}\]

The marginal distribution of the first coin is found in the exact same way \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0) &= \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0 | Y_{i} = 0) \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=0) + \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0 | Y_{i} = 1) \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1) \\ &= 1/2 (1/2) + 1/2 (1/2) = 1/2 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1) &= \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1 | Y_{i} = 0) \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=0) + \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1 | Y_{i} = 1) \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1) \\ &= 1/2 (1/2) + 1/2 (1/2) = 1/2 \end{aligned}\]

NoteMust Know

Now consider a second example, where the second coin is “Completely Unfair”, so that it is always the same as the first. The outcomes generated with a Completely Unfair coin are the same as if we only flipped one coin. \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0, Y_{i} = 0) &= 1/2 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 0, Y_{i} = 1) &= 0 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1, Y_{i} = 0) &= 0 \\ \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1, Y_{i} = 1) &= 1/2 . \end{aligned}\] The joint distribution is written generally as \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y) &= \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x) \mathbf{1}( x=y ), \end{aligned}\] where \(\mathbf{1}(x=y)\) equals \(1\) when \(x=y\) and \(0\) otherwise. The marginal distribution of the second coin is \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 0) &= \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 0 | X_{i} = 0) \text{Prob}(X_{i}=0) + \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 0 | X_{i} = 1) \text{Prob}(X_{i} = 1) \\ &= 1 (1/2) + 0(1/2) = 1/2 . \\ \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 1) &= \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 1 | X_{i} =0) \text{Prob}( X_{i} = 0) + \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = 1 | X_{i} = 1) \text{Prob}( X_{i} = 1) \\ &= 0 (1/2) + 1 (1/2) = 1/2 . \end{aligned}\] which is the same marginal as in the first example!

The marginal distribution of the first coin is found in the exact same way (show this yourself).

Sometimes we observe one variable and want to learn about the other, which reverses the direction of conditioning.

ImportantKey Definition

Bayes’ theorem (Bayes 1763) relates the two conditional directions: \[\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x \mid Y_{i}=y) = \frac{\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=y \mid X_{i}=x)\,\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x)}{\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=y)}.\]

Bayes’ theorem is useful because it is exactly how an observation updates a prior belief. It applies the event version of Bayes’ theorem to random variables. Building on the coin-flip examples above, suppose we observe the outcome of the second coin (\(Y_{i}\)) and want to update our belief about the first coin (\(X_{i}\)).

NoteMust Know

Unfair coins. Now consider the joint distribution from the Test Yourself above, with probs <- c(0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.3):

Two Unfair Coins
\(x=0\) \(x=1\) Marginal
\(y=0\) \(0.4\) \(0.1\) \(0.5\)
\(y=1\) \(0.2\) \(0.3\) \(0.5\)
Marginal \(0.6\) \(0.4\) \(1\)

The prior is \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=1)=0.4\). The conditional probabilities of heads on the second coin are \[ \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1|X_{i}=1) = \frac{0.3}{0.4} = 0.75,\quad \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1|X_{i}=0) = \frac{0.2}{0.6} = \frac{1}{3}. \] Applying Bayes’ theorem, \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i}=1|Y_{i}=1) &= \frac{0.75 \times 0.4}{0.75 \times 0.4 + \frac{1}{3}\times 0.6} = \frac{0.3}{0.3+0.2} = 0.6. \end{aligned}\] The prior probability of heads on the first coin was \(0.4\), but after seeing heads on the second coin, it increases to \(0.6\). The coins are not independent: when the second coin shows heads, it is more likely that the first coin also shows heads.

Definitions for Continuous Data

The joint distribution is defined as \[\begin{aligned} F(x, y) &= \text{Prob}(X_{i} \leq x, Y_{i} \leq y) \end{aligned}\] The marginal distributions are then defined as \[\begin{aligned} F_{X}(x) &= F(x, \infty) \\ F_{Y}(y) &= F(\infty, y). \end{aligned}\] which is called marginalizing (an application of the law of total probability). Variables are statistically independent if \(F(x, y) = F_{X}(x)F_{Y}(y)\) for all \(x, y\).

The most-used parametric joint distribution on continuous data is the bivariate analog of the Normal.

ImportantKey Definition

A bivariate normal distribution describes a random vector \((X_{i}, Y_{i})\) whose joint density is the multivariate normal with mean vector \((\mathbb{E}[X_{i}], \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}])\), variances \((\mathbb{V}[X_{i}], \mathbb{V}[Y_{i}])\), and correlation \(\rho\).

The bivariate normal is useful for analytical work on multivariate random variables. Both marginals are normal, and every conditional \(Y_{i} \mid X_{i} = x\) is also normal with a linear conditional mean. Many results for one Normal random variable therefore extend to the pair.

Code 
library('mvtnorm')

# Simulate Bivariate Data
N <- 10000

NoteMust Know

Independent normal random variables are a special case of the bivariate normal. With both variances equal to \(1\) and \(\rho=0\), drawing \(X_{i}\) and \(Y_{i}\) separately gives the same joint distribution as one draw from the bivariate normal.

18.2 Statistics

Conditional Expectation

The natural population object that regression tries to estimate is the average of \(Y_{i}\) within each subpopulation defined by \(X_{i}\).

ImportantKey Definition

The conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]\) is the population mean of \(Y_{i}\) inside the subpopulation where \(X_{i}=x\): \[\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]=\sum_{y} y\,\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=y\mid X_{i}=x).\]

The conditional expectation is useful as the population target for regression. The sample conditional means \(\hat{m}_{Y}(x)\) from Two Groups estimate \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]\). The fitted line from Simple Regression estimates its best linear approximation. The formula above sums over conditional probabilities for discrete \(X_{i}\); for continuous \(X_{i}\) the conditional expectation function traces a smooth curve indexed by \(x\) (the integral version replaces the sum).

For example, consider a bivariate random vector with three outcomes for \(X_{i}\) and three outcomes for \(Y_{i}\).

Joint Distribution with Three Outcomes Each
\(x=0\) \(x=1\) \(x=2\)
\(y=0\) \(0.0\) \(0.1\) \(0.0\)
\(y=10\) \(0.1\) \(0.3\) \(0.1\)
\(y=20\) \(0.1\) \(0.1\) \(0.2\)

The plot below shows each outcome \((x,y)\) with deeper colors reflecting higher probability events.

We can compute marginals from the table, \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i}=0)=0.2,\quad & \text{Prob}(X_{i}=1)=0.5 ,\quad \text{Prob}(X_{i}=2) = 0.3 \\ \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=0)=0.1,\quad & \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=10)=0.5 ,\quad \text{Prob}(Y_{i}=20) = 0.4 \end{aligned}\]

This gives us conditional probabilities and hence also conditional expectations; \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=0] &= 0\frac{0}{0.2} + 10\frac{0.1}{0.2} + 20\frac{0.1}{0.2} = 0 + \frac{10}{2} + \frac{20}{2} = 15, \\ \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=1] &= 0\frac{0.1}{0.5} + 10\frac{0.3}{0.5} + 20\frac{0.1}{0.5} = 0 + \frac{30}{5} + \frac{20}{5} = 10, \\ \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=2] &= 0\frac{0}{0.3} + 10\frac{0.1}{0.3} + 20\frac{0.2}{0.3} = 0 + \frac{10}{3} + \frac{40}{3} = \frac{50}{3} \approx 16.67. \end{aligned}\] Note that the conditional mean is non-monotonic: \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=0]}{1-0} &= \frac{10 - 15}{1} = -5, \\ \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=2] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=1]}{2-1} &= \frac{\frac{50}{3} - 10}{1} = \frac{20}{3} \approx 6.67. \end{aligned}\] It decreases from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) and then increases from \(x=1\) to \(x=2\).

Code 
# Compute Marginals
P_x <- colSums(P_xy) #Prob(X_i=x)
P_y <- rowSums(P_xy) #Prob(Y_i=y)

# Compute the conditional probabilities Prob(Y_i=y | X_i=x).
PY_x <- matrix(0, nrow = 3, ncol = 3)
for (j in seq(ncol(P_xy)) ) {
  PY_x[, j] <- P_xy[, j] / P_x[j]

Covariance

We can also dig a little deeper into the other bivariate statistics we compute. When we know how the data are generated, we can often compute the foundational covariance statistic. The covariance is the expected product of deviations from the population means: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}] &= \mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i} - \mathbb{E}[X_{i}])(Y_{i} - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}])\right] \\ &= \sum_{x}\sum_{y} (x - \mathbb{E}[X_{i}])(y - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]) \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y) \end{aligned}\]

Using the marginal distributions from before allows us to compute the means: \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X_{i}] &= 0(0.2)+1(0.5)+2(0.3) = 1.1 \\ \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}] &= 0(0.1)+10(0.5)+20(0.4) = 13 \end{aligned}\] We can then compute the cell-by-cell contributions: \(\text{Prob}(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y) (x-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])(y-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}])\), which plug in to the covariance formula; \[ \begin{array}{l l r r r r r} \hline x & y & \text{Prob}(X_{i}=x, Y_{i}=y) & x-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}] & y-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}] & (x-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])(y-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]) & \text{Contribution}\\ \hline 0 & 0 & 0.0 & -1.1 & -13 & 14.3 & 0\\ 0 & 10 & 0.1 & -1.1 & -3 & 3.3 & 0.330\\ 0 & 20 & 0.1 & -1.1 & 7 & -7.7 & -0.770\\ 1 & 0 & 0.1 & -0.1 & -13 & 1.3 & 0.130\\ 1 & 10 & 0.3 & -0.1 & -3 & 0.3 & 0.090\\ 1 & 20 & 0.1 & -0.1 & 7 & -0.7 & -0.070\\ 2 & 0 & 0.0 & 0.9 & -13 & -11.7 & 0\\ 2 & 10 & 0.1 & 0.9 & -3 & -2.7 & -0.270\\ 2 & 20 & 0.2 & 0.9 & 7 & 6.3 & 1.260\\ \hline \end{array} \] \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}[X_{i},Y_{i}] &= \sum_{x} \sum_{y} \left(x-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\right)\left(y-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]\right) \text{Prob}\left(X_{i} = x, Y_{i} = y\right) \\ &= 0 + 0.330 -0.770 + 0.130 + 0.090 -0.070 +0 -0.270 + 1.260 = 0.7 \end{aligned}\]

Note that you can do the above calculations using the computer instead of by hand.

We can now use the covariance to compute the often-used Pearson correlation.

NoteMust Know

The Pearson correlation (Pearson 1896) divides the covariance by the product of the two standard deviations: \[ \frac{\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}] }{ \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]} \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[Y_{i}]} } \]

To compute this statistic in the previous example, we first need the variances \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{V}[X_{i}] &= \sum_{x} (x-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])^2 \text{Prob}(X_{i} = x) \\ &= (0-1.1)^2(0.2)+(1-1.1)^2(0.5)+(2-1.1)^2(0.3)=0.49 \\ \mathbb{V}[Y_{i}] &= \sum_{y} (y-\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}])^2 \text{Prob}(Y_{i} = y) \\ &= (0-13)^2(0.1)+(10-13)^2(0.5)+(20-13)^2(0.4)=41 \end{aligned}\] Then we can find the correlation as \[\begin{aligned} \frac{\mathbb{C}[X_{i},Y_{i}]}{ \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]} \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[Y_{i}]} } &= \frac{0.7}{\sqrt{0.49} \sqrt{41}} = \frac{0.7}{0.7 \sqrt{41}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 0.156, \end{aligned}\] which suggests a weak positive association between the variables.

VY <- sum( (y-EY)^2 * P_y)

Regression Coefficient

Just as the sample slope \(\hat{b}_{1}\) summarized a sample, its population counterpart describes how the data are generated.

ImportantKey Definition

A linear data generating process is \[Y_{i} = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1} X_{i} + \epsilon_{i}, \quad \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i} \mid X_{i}=x] = 0 \text{ for every } x,\] where the fixed numbers \(\beta_{0}\) and \(\beta_{1}\) are the (theoretical) regression coefficients and \(\epsilon_{i}\) is the population error term. In this model, the slope equals the covariance over the variance of \(X_{i}\): \[\beta_{1} = \frac{\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}] }{ \mathbb{V}[X_{i}] }.\]

To see why, note that the error has mean zero at every \(x\), so it also has mean zero overall, and \(Y_{i} - \mathbb{E}[Y_{i}] = \beta_{1}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]) + \epsilon_{i}\). Then \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}] &= \mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])\left(\beta_{1}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]) + \epsilon_{i}\right)\right] \\ &= \beta_{1}\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])^2\right] + \mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])\epsilon_{i}\right] \\ &= \beta_{1}\mathbb{V}[X_{i}], \end{aligned}\] where the last term is zero because \(\epsilon_{i}\) averages to zero at each value of \(X_{i}\). Dividing both sides by \(\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]\) gives the formula in the definition.

\(\beta_{1}\) is useful as the population target of the sample slope \(\hat{b}_{1} = \hat{c}_{XY}/\hat{v}_{X}\) from Simple Regression: when the data come from a linear data generating process, OLS estimates \(\beta_{1}\) from a finite sample. In that case, the conditional expectation is itself a straight line, \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x] = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x\), because the error averages to zero at every \(x\). However, the ratio \(\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}]/\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]\) can be computed for any joint distribution, even when no linear data generating process exists.

NoteMust Know

For the \(3\times3\) table above, the conditional expectation computed earlier is not a straight line (it falls from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\), then rises), so the data are not generated by a linear model and there is no true \(\beta_{1}\). We already found \(\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}] = 0.7\) and \(\mathbb{V}[X_{i}] = 0.49\), so the ratio is still \[ \frac{\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}]}{\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]} = \frac{0.7}{0.49} = \frac{70}{49} = \frac{10}{7} \approx 1.43. \] This is the slope of the best straight-line approximation to the conditional expectation. The line is “best” in the sense that it minimizes mean squared error among all linear approximations to the conditional expectation function. OLS estimates this slope from samples of the table, and it is positive because the line captures the overall upward tendency. But when the conditional expectation bends, the best line still misses: here it misses the dip at \(x=1\).

18.3 Type I and Type II Errors

A hypothesis test starts with a null \(H_{0}\) and an alternative \(H_{A}\), and produces a decision (reject or fail-to-reject) from finite sample data. Because the data have variability, the decision can be wrong in two ways (Neyman and Pearson 1933).

ImportantKey Definition

A Type I error is rejecting \(H_{0}\) when \(H_{0}\) is true (a false positive). A Type II error is failing to reject \(H_{0}\) when \(H_{0}\) is false (a false negative).

This distinction is useful because the two error types are managed by different levers: we control Type I error by setting the significance level \(\alpha=\text{Prob}(\text{Type I})\) before looking at the data, while Type II error is harder to control directly and depends on the sample size and the true effect size. Reducing one error rate usually raises the other for a fixed sample.

Type I and Type II Errors
True Situation Decision: Fail to Reject \(H_{0}\) Decision: Reject \(H_{0}\)
\(H_{0}\) is True Correct (no detection) Type I Error (False Positive)
\(H_{0}\) is False Type II Error (False Negative; missed detection) Correct (effect detected)
TipTest Yourself

Here is a Courtroom example: Someone suspected of committing a crime is at trial, and they are either guilty or not (a Bernoulli random variable). You hypothesize that the suspect is innocent, and a jury can either convict them (decide guilty) or free them (decide not-guilty). Recall that fail-to-reject a hypothesis does not mean accepting it, so deciding not-guilty does not necessarily mean innocent.

Courtroom Decisions
True Situation Decision: Free Decision: Convict
Suspect Innocent Correctly Freed Falsely Convicted
Suspect Guilty Falsely Freed Correctly Convicted

Adamson and Rentschler (2025) build a similar “truth table of criminal justice” for policing. Each civilian either steals or produces, and each can be punished or not, which gives true and false positives and negatives.

Statistical Power

The complement of Type II error has its own name because it is what we actually want from the test.

ImportantKey Definition

Statistical power \(1-\beta\) is the probability of correctly rejecting \(H_{0}\) when \(H_{0}\) is false: \[\text{Power} = 1 - \beta = 1 - \text{Prob}(\text{Type II Error}).\] (Here \(\beta\) is the Type II error probability, not the regression coefficient \(\beta_{1}\).)

Power is useful as the probability that a true effect of a given size will actually show up as a rejection in our data: it rises with the sample size \(n\), the true effect size, and the significance level \(\alpha\). The complementary error rate, the significance level \(\alpha = \text{Prob}(\text{Type I Error})\), is something we choose in advance (commonly \(\alpha=0.05\)). Power is less often chosen directly; it follows from the chosen sample size and the assumed effect size, and lets us judge whether a study is well-powered to detect what we care about (Cohen 1988; Ioannidis et al. 2017).

TipTest Yourself

The code below runs a small simulation using a shifted, nonparametric bootstrap. It uses a two-sided test with a studentized statistic for \(H_{0}: \mathbb{E}[X_{i}] = 0\).

Code 
# Power for Two-sided test;
# nonparametric bootstrap, studentized statistic
n <- 25
null_mean <- 0
alpha <- 0.05
n_boot <- 299

sim_reps <- 100

p_values <- rep(NA, sim_reps)
for (i in seq(p_values)) {
    # Generate data
    x_sim <- rnorm(n, mean=0.2, sd=1)
    # Observed statistic
    m_hat <- mean(x_sim)
    T_obs <-  (m_hat - null_mean) / (sd(x_sim)/ sqrt(n)) ##studentized
    # Bootstrap null distribution of the statistic
    T_boot <- rep(NA, n_boot)
    x_null_sim <- x_sim - m_hat + null_mean # Impose the null by recentering
    for (b in seq(T_boot)) {
      x_boot <- sample(x_null_sim, size = n, replace = TRUE)
      T_b <- (mean(x_boot) - null_mean) / (sd(x_boot)/sqrt(n))
      T_boot[b] <- T_b
    }
    # Two-sided bootstrap p-value
    pval <- mean(abs(T_boot) >= abs(T_obs))
    p_values[i] <- pval
    }
power <- mean(p_values < alpha)
power

There is an important Trade-off for fixed sample sizes: Increasing significance (fewer false positives) often lowers power (more false negatives). Generally, power depends on the effect size and sample size: bigger true effects and larger \(n\) make it easier to detect real differences (higher power, lower \(\beta\)).

Regression Slope Example

Consider testing \(H_{0}: \beta_{1} = 0\) for the regression of hourly wage on years of schooling. As in Sampling, pretend the \(3294\) workers in Wages1 are the whole population, so we know the true slope and whether the null is true. When the true slope is nonzero (null is false), failing to reject is a Type II Error, and the share of samples that reject estimates the power. The population slope is population_slope, about \(0.557\), so the null is false. Each sample is tested with the permutation test from Simple Regression, which shuffles wages across workers.

## 0.5571617

# Function: draw one sample of n workers, return the permutation p-value for the slope
sample_pval <- function(n, population, n_perm=199) {
    s_id <- sample(nrow(population), n, replace=FALSE)
    x_sim <- population[s_id, 'x']
    y_sim <- population[s_id, 'y']
    # Slope as covariance over variance, which is much faster than lm() inside a loop
    b1_hat <- cov(x_sim, y_sim) / var(x_sim)
    b1_perm <- rep(NA, n_perm)
    for (b in seq_along(b1_perm)) {
        y_perm <- sample(y_sim, replace=FALSE) # breaks any link with schooling, imposing the null
        b1_perm[b] <- cov(x_sim, y_perm) / var(x_sim)
    }
    # Two-sided p-value: share of permuted slopes at least as far from zero
    p_value <- mean(abs(b1_perm) >= abs(b1_hat))
    return(p_value)
}

sim_reps <- 300
alpha <- 0.05

    p_values <- replicate(sim_reps, sample_pval(n_grid[k], population_xy))

18.4 Bayesian Updating

The coin flips example used Bayes’ theorem to update a prior belief about one coin after seeing the other. The same formula turns a test result into an updated belief about whether a hypothesis is true.

Each term in the formula has a name:

  • \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x)\) is the prior probability for \(X_{i}=x\).
  • \(\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=y|X_{i}=x)\) is the likelihood of seeing \(Y_{i}=y\) if \(X_{i}=x\) is true.
  • \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x|Y_{i}=y)\) is the posterior, your updated probability after seeing \(Y_{i}=y\).

A useful way to remember the structure is “Posterior \(\propto\) Likelihood \(\times\) Prior”. For two states, the posterior odds equal the prior odds times a likelihood ratio.

Bayesian Screening

A common application of Bayes’ theorem is interpreting a positive result from a diagnostic test.

ImportantKey Definition

For a binary screening test where \(X_{i}=1\) means “condition present” and \(Y_{i}=1\) means “test positive”:

Prevalence \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=1)\) is the base rate of the condition in the population.

Sensitivity \(\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1\mid X_{i}=1)\) is the probability of a positive result when the condition is present.

False positive rate \(\text{Prob}(Y_{i}=1\mid X_{i}=0)\) is the probability of a positive result when it is absent.

Screening is useful for converting test accuracy and base rates into the quantity an individual actually cares about: the posterior \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=1\mid Y_{i}=1)\), the probability of having the condition given a positive result. The three inputs play different roles: sensitivity and the false positive rate describe the test, while prevalence describes the population. Suppose a screening test has sensitivity \(0.90\), false positive rate \(0.08\), and prevalence \(0.12\). Applying Bayes’ theorem, \[\begin{aligned} \text{Prob}(X_{i}=1|Y_{i}=1) &= \frac{0.90\times0.12}{0.90\times0.12 + 0.08\times0.88} \approx 0.605. \end{aligned}\] Even with a good test, the posterior depends strongly on prevalence: rare conditions produce many false alarms even when the test is accurate. The fraud-detection example in Bivariate Distributions showed the same pattern with counts: the same flag meant fraud was unlikely when fraud was rare and likely when it was common.

Code 
# States: X in {0, 1}, signal Y in {0, 1}
# Prior Prob(X_i=1)
p_x1 <- 0.12

# Test characteristics
p_y1_x1 <- 0.90
p_y1_x0 <- 0.08

# Law of total probability for Prob(Y_i=1)
p_y1 <- p_y1_x1 * p_x1 + p_y1_x0 * (1 - p_x1)

# Bayes posterior Prob(X_i=1 | Y_i=1)
p_x1_y1 <- (p_y1_x1 * p_x1) / p_y1
p_x1_y1
## [1] 0.6053812

# Also compute Prob(X_i=1 | Y_i=0)
p_y0_x1 <- 1 - p_y1_x1
p_y0_x0 <- 1 - p_y1_x0
p_y0 <- p_y0_x1 * p_x1 + p_y0_x0 * (1 - p_x1)
p_x1_y0 <- (p_y0_x1 * p_x1) / p_y0
p_x1_y0
## [1] 0.01460565
TipTest Yourself

Notice that a positive test result raises the probability of having the condition from \(12\%\) (prior) to about \(60\%\) (posterior). A negative test result lowers it from \(12\%\) to about \(1.5\%\). If prevalence were higher, say \(50\%\), the posterior after a positive test would be \(\frac{0.90 \times 0.50}{0.90 \times 0.50 + 0.08 \times 0.50} = \frac{0.45}{0.49} \approx 0.92\). The same test is far more informative when prevalence is higher.

Posterior for a Regression Slope

The screening test has the same structure as testing a regression slope. Let \(X_{i}=1\) mean “slope is truly nonzero” and \(Y_{i}=1\) mean “the test rejects \(H_{0}\).” Then sensitivity equals power, the false positive rate equals \(\alpha\), and prevalence is your prior belief that the slope is nonzero. Using the simulation results from the previous section:

Code 
# Prior: 50-50 chance the slope is nonzero
prior_nonzero <- 0.5

# 'Sensitivity' = power, 'False positive rate' = alpha
p_reject <- power_est * prior_nonzero + alpha * (1 - prior_nonzero)

# Posterior: Prob(slope nonzero | test rejects)
post_nonzero <- (power_est * prior_nonzero) / p_reject
post_nonzero
##        50 
## 0.9157303

    (power_est * prior_grid + alpha * (1 - prior_grid))

    xlab = 'Prior Prob(slope nonzero)',
    ylab = 'Posterior Prob(slope nonzero | reject)',

Recall

This chapter defined joint, marginal, and conditional distributions for a random vector, and the bivariate normal. It also defined the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]\), the theoretical covariance \(\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}]\) and correlation, and the regression coefficient \(\beta_{1}\) of a linear data generating process. It then used conditional probabilities for the Type I / Type II error distinction with statistical power, and Bayes’ theorem for converting a test rejection into a posterior. The \(3\times 3\) worked example with \(\text{Prob}(X_{i}=x, Y_{i}=y)\) across \(x~\text{in}~\{0, 1, 2\}\) and \(y~\text{in}~\{0, 10, 20\}\) produced a non-monotonic conditional expectation. It fell from \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=0]=15\) to \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=1]=10\) and rose to \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}=2]=50/3\), so there is no true \(\beta_{1}\). But the ratio \(\mathbb{C}[X_{i}, Y_{i}]/\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]=10/7\) still gives an upward best-fitting slope. The example shows the gap between \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]\) and its best linear approximation. The unfair-coin example showed Bayes’ theorem at work: heads on the second coin raised the probability of heads on the first from \(0.4\) to \(0.6\). Treating the Wages1 workers as a population showed both error rates. Samples from the real population estimated power across sample sizes \(n~\text{in}~\{10, 20, 50, 100, 200\}\). Samples from the shuffled population rejected at a rate near \(\alpha\).
