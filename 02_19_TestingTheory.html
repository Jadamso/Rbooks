19  Classical Tests and Bias-Variance

Earlier chapters tested hypotheses with resampling. This chapter develops the theoretical counterpart: when the data are well-behaved, the sampling distribution of a \(t\)- or \(z\)-statistic is known in advance, so we can test means, medians, and regression slopes with formulas. With the conditional expectation as the target, the bias-variance tradeoff then describes how regression estimates miss it.

19.1 Classical Tests

Just as with one sample tests, we can compute standardized differences. The observed difference is converted into a \(t\)-statistic by dividing by an estimated standard error. Note, however, that we have to compute the standard error for the difference statistic, which is a bit more complicated than for a single sample. Under the assumption that both populations are independently distributed, we can analytically derive the sampling distribution for the differences between two groups.

Differences in Means

Let \(Y_{ig}\) denote the random outcome for individual \(i\) in group \(g\). To compare two group means, we standardize the observed difference by its estimated standard error.

ImportantKey Definition

The two-sample t-statistic divides the observed mean difference by its estimated standard error: \[\hat{t} = \frac{\hat{m}_{1} - \hat{m}_{2}}{\sqrt{\hat{s}^2_{1}/n_{1}+\hat{s}^2_{2}/n_{2}}},\] where \(\hat{s}^2_{g}\) is the sample variance and \(n_{g}\) is the sample size for group \(g\).

Welch’s t-test (Welch 1947) modifies the two-sample \(\hat{t}\) above to account for unequal variances or unequal sample sizes. The modifications use the Welch-Satterthwaite approximation.

The two-sample \(\hat{t}\) is useful as a standardized test statistic for comparing two means. Dividing by the standard error puts the gap in standard-error units. So the same numerator can be large or small in \(\hat{t}\) units, depending on \(n_{1}, n_{2}\) and the within-group variances. With normally distributed group means, this statistic follows Student’s t-distribution (Student 1908), so we can read a \(p\)-value from a formula rather than from resampling. When the two groups have unequal variances or sample sizes, a small adjustment to the degrees of freedom keeps the \(t\)-distribution approximation accurate.

The \(t\)-distribution result needs normal data, but in large samples the central limit theorem makes the difference in means approximately normal whatever the shape of the data.

ImportantKey Definition

The \(z\)-statistic standardizes an estimate by its estimated standard error, as \(\hat{t}\) does, but compares the result to the standard normal rather than to a \(t\)-distribution. For the difference statistic \(\hat{d}\) and the null of no difference, \[\hat{z} = \frac{\hat{d}}{\hat{\text{SE}}(D)}.\]

The \(z\)-statistic is useful because it only needs the estimate to be approximately normal, which holds in large samples for many statistics besides the mean. For means, \(\hat{d}=\hat{m}_{1}-\hat{m}_{2}\) and \(\hat{\text{SE}}(D)=\sqrt{\hat{s}^2_{1}/n_{1}+\hat{s}^2_{2}/n_{2}}\), so \(\hat{z}\) is the same number as the two-sample \(\hat{t}\); only the reference distribution changes. The two-sided \(p\)-value is the probability that a standard normal draw lands at least \(|\hat{z}|\) from zero, which R computes as 2 * pnorm(-abs(z_hat)). The standard normal has thinner tails than any \(t\)-distribution, so the \(z\) \(p\)-value is smaller, but the gap shrinks as the groups grow.

For example, we can compare the mean hourly wage of women and men in the Wages1 data. The sex variable has levels female and male, in that order, so the reported difference is the female mean minus the male mean.

Code 
data(Wages1, package='Ecdat')
levels(Wages1[, 'sex'])
## [1] "female" "male"

# Welch's t-test (the default for t.test)
t.test(wage ~ sex, data=Wages1)
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
## 
## data:  wage by sex
## t = -10.485, df = 3259.3, p-value < 2.2e-16
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group female and group male is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -1.384162 -0.948033
## sample estimates:
## mean in group female   mean in group male 
##             5.146924             6.313021

The reported statistic is also \(\hat{z}\approx -10.5\). With more than \(1500\) workers per group, the \(t\) and \(z\) \(p\)-values are both essentially zero.

NoteMust Know

The same test works on simulated data, where we choose the group means ourselves. Here the two groups have different means and different variances.

Code 
# Sample 1
n1 <- 100
y1_sim <- rnorm(n1, 0, 2)
#hist(y1_sim, freq=F, main='Sample 1')

# Sample 2
n2 <- 80
y2_sim <- rnorm(n2, 1, 1)
#hist(y2_sim, freq=F, main='Sample 2')

t.test(y1_sim, y2_sim, var.equal=FALSE)
## 
##  Welch Two Sample t-test
## 
## data:  y1_sim and y2_sim
## t = -3.6939, df = 158.27, p-value = 0.0003037
## alternative hypothesis: true difference in means is not equal to 0
## 95 percent confidence interval:
##  -1.190604 -0.360992
## sample estimates:
## mean of x mean of y 
## 0.2177747 0.9935727

Welch’s \(t\)-test is useful as the default for comparing two means without assuming equal variances. Unequal variances are common in applied work, for example when comparing two job markets or two countries. With the variance-and-normality assumptions in place, the test can be conducted entirely with formulas (no bootstrap resampling required). Compare the bootstrap test of a mean difference in Two Groups, which needed neither assumption.

NoteMust Know

The Welch-Satterthwaite equation (Satterthwaite 1946; Welch 1947) approximates the degrees of freedom when the two groups have unequal variances: \[ \text{df} = \frac{\left(\hat{s}^2_{1}/n_{1} + \hat{s}^2_{2}/n_{2}\right)^2}{\frac{(\hat{s}^2_{1}/n_{1})^2}{n_{1}-1} + \frac{(\hat{s}^2_{2}/n_{2})^2}{n_{2}-1}}. \] For example, suppose both groups have \(n_{1}=n_{2}=10\), with sample variances \(\hat{s}^2_{1}=4\) and \(\hat{s}^2_{2}=1\). Writing \(v_{g}=\hat{s}^2_{g}/n_{g}\), we have \(v_{1}=0.4\) and \(v_{2}=0.1\), so \[ \text{df} = \frac{(0.4+0.1)^2}{0.4^2/9 + 0.1^2/9} = \frac{0.25}{0.17/9} \approx 13.2. \] This is below the equal-variance value \(n_{1}+n_{2}-2=18\): unequal variances cost degrees of freedom, which widens the \(t\)-distribution and makes the test more conservative.

Code 
n01 <- 10; n02 <- 10
v01_hat <- 4/n01
v02_hat <- 1/n02
(v01_hat + v02_hat)^2 / (v01_hat^2/(n01-1) + v02_hat^2/(n02-1))
## [1] 13.23529
NoteMust Know

Continue the Welch-Satterthwaite example, with \(n_{1}=n_{2}=10\), \(\hat{s}^2_{1}=4\), and \(\hat{s}^2_{2}=1\), and suppose \(\hat{d}=\hat{m}_{1}-\hat{m}_{2}=1.5\). Then \(\hat{\text{SE}}(D)=\sqrt{0.4+0.1}=\sqrt{0.5}\approx 0.71\), so \(\hat{z}=\hat{t}=1.5/\sqrt{0.5}\approx 2.12\). The standard normal gives a \(p\)-value of about \(0.034\), while the \(t\)-distribution with \(13.2\) degrees of freedom gives about \(0.053\). With only \(10\) observations per group, the two reference distributions disagree about rejecting at the \(5\%\) level.

Code 
# The two groups from the Welch-Satterthwaite example, with a mean difference of 1.5
d0_hat <- 1.5
se0_hat <- sqrt(v01_hat + v02_hat)
z0_hat <- d0_hat / se0_hat # the same number as the two-sample t-statistic
df0 <- (v01_hat + v02_hat)^2 / (v01_hat^2/(n01 - 1) + v02_hat^2/(n02 - 1))

# Same statistic, two reference distributions
c(z=z0_hat,
    p_normal=2 * pnorm(-abs(z0_hat)),
    p_t=2 * pt(-abs(z0_hat), df=df0))
##          z   p_normal        p_t 
## 2.12132034 0.03389485 0.05332678

We can visualize this by simulating many samples under the null (\(\mathbb{E}[Y_{i1}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i2}]\)) and comparing the distribution of \(\hat{t}\) with the theoretical \(t\)-distribution (dashed curve) and the standard normal used for \(\hat{z}\) (dotted curve). As in Sampling, pretend the \(3294\) workers in Wages1 are the whole population. If we draw both groups from the same population of wages, then the null of equal means holds exactly.

Code 
# Pretend these 3294 workers are the whole population
data(Wages1, package='Ecdat')
population_xy <- Wages1[, c('school', 'wage')]
colnames(population_xy) <- c('x', 'y') # x=years of schooling, y=hourly wage
population_wages <- population_xy[, 'y']

# Simulate the t-statistic under the null (both groups from the same population)
n1 <- 100
n2 <- 80
n_sim <- 399
t_sim <- vector(length = n_sim)
for (b in seq(n_sim)) {
    y1_sim_b <- population_wages[sample(length(population_wages), n1, replace=FALSE)]
    y2_sim_b <- population_wages[sample(length(population_wages), n2, replace=FALSE)]
    t_sim[b] <- t.test(y1_sim_b, y2_sim_b, var.equal = FALSE)$statistic
}

# Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
# (use the population variance for both groups)
v1 <- var(population_wages) / n1
v2 <- var(population_wages) / n2
df_ws <- (v1 + v2)^2 / (v1^2 / (n1 - 1) + v2^2 / (n2 - 1))

# Overlay: simulated histogram vs. theoretical t-distribution
t_range <- range(c(t_sim, -4, 4))
hist(t_sim, breaks = 30, freq = FALSE, border = NA,
    xlim = t_range,
    xlab = expression(hat(t)),
    main = NA)
title('Simulated vs. Theoretical Distributions', font.main = 1)
t_grid <- seq(t_range[1], t_range[2], length = 200)
lines(t_grid, dt(t_grid, df = df_ws), col = rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty = 2)
lines(t_grid, dnorm(t_grid), col = rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty = 3) # standard normal, for z
legend('topright', legend = c('Simulated', paste0('t(', round(df_ws), ')'), 'Standard normal'),
    fill = c(grey(.83), NA, NA), border = NA, # grey(.83) matches the default histogram fill
    lwd = c(NA, 1, 1), lty = c(NA, 2, 3), col = c(NA, rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), rgb(0, 0, 1, .8)), bty = 'n')

Wages are right-skewed, so the group means are not exactly normal and the \(t\)-distribution is only an approximation. With groups of \(100\) and \(80\) workers, the histogram is typically close to both curves. The two curves nearly coincide because the \(t\)-distribution has about \(170\) degrees of freedom here, so \(\hat{z}\) gives almost the same \(p\)-value as \(\hat{t}\). With much smaller groups, the approximation can be noticeably worse, and the fatter tails of the \(t\)-distribution pull away from the standard normal.

NoteMust Know

We can also simulate under the null with normally distributed data, where the \(t\)-distribution result applies more directly. Here the two groups have the same mean but different variances.

Code 
# Simulate the t-statistic under the null (both groups same mean)
n_sim <- 399
t_sim_norm <- vector(length = n_sim)
for (b in seq(n_sim)) {
    y1_sim_b <- rnorm(n1, 0, 2)
    y2_sim_b <- rnorm(n2, 0, 1)
    t_sim_norm[b] <- t.test(y1_sim_b, y2_sim_b, var.equal = FALSE)$statistic
}

# Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
# (use population variances as approximation)
v1_norm <- 2^2 / n1
v2_norm <- 1^2 / n2
df_ws_norm <- (v1_norm + v2_norm)^2 / (v1_norm^2 / (n1 - 1) + v2_norm^2 / (n2 - 1))

# Overlay: simulated histogram vs. theoretical t-distribution
t_range <- range(c(t_sim_norm, -4, 4))
hist(t_sim_norm, breaks = 30, freq = FALSE, border = NA,
    xlim = t_range,
    xlab = expression(hat(t)),
    main = NA)
title('Simulated vs. Theoretical Distributions', font.main = 1)
t_grid <- seq(t_range[1], t_range[2], length = 200)
lines(t_grid, dt(t_grid, df = df_ws_norm), col = rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty = 2)
lines(t_grid, dnorm(t_grid), col = rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty = 3) # standard normal, for z
legend('topright', legend = c('Simulated', paste0('t(', round(df_ws_norm), ')'), 'Standard normal'),
    fill = c(grey(.83), NA, NA), border = NA, # grey(.83) matches the default histogram fill
    lwd = c(NA, 1, 1), lty = c(NA, 2, 3), col = c(NA, rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), rgb(0, 0, 1, .8)), bty = 'n')

Regression Slope

The same ideas apply to regression coefficients. Recall that the OLS slope \(\hat{b}_{1} = \hat{c}_{XY}/\hat{v}_{X}\) is a statistic computed from the data. In the Simple Regression chapter, we estimated variability using data-driven methods (jackknife and bootstrap). Under standard assumptions, we can instead derive the sampling distribution analytically: the \(t\)-statistic \[ \hat{t} = \frac{\hat{b}_{1}}{\hat{\text{SE}}(B_{1})} \] follows a \(t\)-distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom, where \(\hat{\text{SE}}(B_{1})\) is the classical estimated standard error of the slope. This allows us to test \(H_{0}: \beta_{1} = 0\) using math alone. In large samples, the OLS slope is approximately normal even when the errors are not, so the same ratio can also be read as a \(z\)-statistic, \(\hat{z}=\hat{b}_{1}/\hat{\text{SE}}(B_{1})\), and compared to the standard normal.

Code 
# USArrests data (same as Simple Regression chapter)
xy_hat <- USArrests[, c('UrbanPop', 'Murder')]
colnames(xy_hat) <- c('x_hat', 'y_hat') # x_hat=urban population share, y_hat=murder arrests per 100K
reg <- lm(y_hat ~ x_hat, data = xy_hat)

# The summary reports theoretical t-statistics and p-values
summary(reg)$coefficients
##               Estimate Std. Error   t value   Pr(>|t|)
## (Intercept) 6.41594246 2.90669257 2.2073000 0.03210725
## x_hat       0.02093466 0.04332647 0.4831841 0.63116178
NoteMust Know

Manual verification of the theoretical \(t\)-statistic for the slope:

Code 
n <- nrow(xy_hat)
b1_hat <- coef(reg)[2]
e_hat <- resid(reg)
s_e_hat <- sqrt(sum(e_hat^2) / (n - 2))               # residual standard error
s_b1 <- s_e_hat / sqrt(sum((xy_hat[, 'x_hat'] - mean(xy_hat[, 'x_hat']))^2)) # SE of slope
t_stat <- b1_hat / s_b1

# Compare with summary output
c(manual = t_stat, summary = summary(reg)$coefficients[2, 3])
## manual.x_hat      summary 
##    0.4831841    0.4831841

We can compare the bootstrap sampling distribution of \(\hat{t}\) (histogram) with the theoretical \(t_{n-2}\) distribution (dashed curve) and the standard normal (dotted curve). Each bootstrap \(\hat{t}\) is centered at the original slope estimate, which plays the role of the true slope in the bootstrap world. So the histogram approximates the distribution of \(\hat{t}\) when the null is true. They should be similar, but are not identical because the theoretical result assumes normally distributed errors.

Code 
# Bootstrap distribution of the t-statistic
n <- nrow(xy_hat)
b1_hat <- coef(reg)[2]
n_boot <- 399
boot_t <- vector(length = n_boot)
for (b in seq(n_boot)) {
    b_id <- sample(nrow(xy_hat), replace = TRUE)
    xy_boot <- xy_hat[b_id, ]
    reg_b <- lm(y_hat ~ x_hat, data = xy_boot)
    # Center at the original estimate and use the theoretical SE from summary
    coef_b <- summary(reg_b)$coefficients[2, ]
    boot_t[b] <- (coef_b[1] - b1_hat) / coef_b[2]
}

# Overlay: bootstrap histogram vs. theoretical t-distribution
t_range <- range(c(boot_t, -4, 4))
hist(boot_t, breaks = 30, freq = FALSE, border = NA,
    xlim = t_range,
    xlab = expression(hat(t)),
    main = NA)
title('Bootstrap vs. Theoretical Distributions', font.main = 1)
# Theoretical t(n-2) density, and the standard normal for z
t_grid <- seq(t_range[1], t_range[2], length = 200)
lines(t_grid, dt(t_grid, df = n - 2), col = rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty = 2)
lines(t_grid, dnorm(t_grid), col = rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty = 3)
legend('topright', legend = c('Bootstrap', 't(n-2)', 'Standard normal'),
    fill = c(grey(.83), NA, NA), border = NA, # grey(.83) matches the default histogram fill
    lwd = c(NA, 1, 1), lty = c(NA, 2, 3), col = c(NA, rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), rgb(0, 0, 1, .8)), bty = 'n')

We can compute the \(p\)-value for \(H_{0}: \beta_{1} = 0\) three ways: from the theoretical \(t\)-distribution, from the standard normal, and from the bootstrap distribution. All three ask how extreme the observed \(\hat{t}\) is under the null.

Code 
t_obs <- summary(reg)$coefficients[2, 3]

# Theoretical p-value: Prob(|t| >= |t_obs|) under t(n-2)
p_theory <- 2 * pt(-abs(t_obs), df = n - 2)

# Normal p-value: the same ratio read as a z-statistic
p_normal <- 2 * pnorm(-abs(t_obs))

# Bootstrap p-value: fraction of bootstrap |t|'s >= |t_obs|
p_boot <- mean(abs(boot_t) >= abs(t_obs))

c(t=p_theory, z=p_normal, bootstrap=p_boot)
##         t         z bootstrap 
## 0.6311618 0.6289650 0.6315789

With \(n=50\) states, the \(t_{48}\) distribution is already close to the standard normal, so the \(t\) and \(z\) \(p\)-values agree to two decimal places.

The same ratio can test other hypothesized values \(\beta_{1}\) of the population slope. To impose the null, recenter the numerator around the hypothetical value: \(\hat{t} = \frac{\hat{b}_{1} - \beta_{1}}{\hat{\text{SE}}(B_{1})}\), as we did for the mean in \(t\)-values. Under the same assumptions as above, this \(\hat{t}\) also follows a \(t\)-distribution with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom when the null is true.

TipTest Yourself

Redo the bootstrap \(p\)-value with the jackknife standard error in place of the classical one. Recompute the jackknife standard error inside each bootstrap resample.

Code 
# Jackknife SE of the slope and the observed t
jack_coefs <- rep(NA, n)
for (i in seq_along(jack_coefs)) {
    xy_i <- xy_hat[-i, ]
    jack_coefs[i] <- coef(lm(y_hat ~ x_hat, data = xy_i))[2]
}
jack_se <- sqrt((n - 1)/n * sum((jack_coefs - mean(jack_coefs))^2))
t_jack <- b1_hat / jack_se

# Bootstrap t, with a jackknife SE for each resample
boot_t_jack <- rep(NA, n_boot)
for (b in seq(n_boot)) {
    b_id <- sample(n, replace = TRUE)
    xy_boot <- xy_hat[b_id, ]
    slope_b <- coef(lm(y_hat ~ x_hat, data = xy_boot))[2]
    jack_coefs_b <- rep(NA, n)
    for (i in seq_along(jack_coefs_b)) {
        xy_boot_i <- xy_boot[-i, ]
        jack_coefs_b[i] <- coef(lm(y_hat ~ x_hat, data = xy_boot_i))[2]
    }
    jack_se_b <- sqrt((n - 1)/n * sum((jack_coefs_b - mean(jack_coefs_b))^2))
    # Center at the original estimate, as above
    boot_t_jack[b] <- (slope_b - b1_hat) / jack_se_b
}

# Classical t p-value next to the bootstrap p-value with jackknife SEs
c(classical=p_theory, jackknife_boot=mean(abs(boot_t_jack) >= abs(t_jack)))
##      classical jackknife_boot 
##      0.6311618      0.6190476

Checking with a Known Population

A \(95\%\) confidence interval should contain the population slope in about \(95\%\) of samples. With real data, we never see the population slope, so we cannot check whether our intervals work. As in Sampling, pretend the \(3294\) workers in Wages1 are the whole population, so we know the population slope of wages on schooling. We then draw many samples of \(n=100\) workers, fit a regression on each, and see how the sample slopes scatter around the population slope.

Code 
# Pretend these 3294 workers are the whole population
data(Wages1, package='Ecdat')
population_xy <- Wages1[, c('school', 'wage')]
colnames(population_xy) <- c('x', 'y') # x=years of schooling, y=hourly wage
population_slope <- coef(lm(y ~ x, data=population_xy))[2]
population_slope
##         x 
## 0.5571617

# Draw many samples and fit a regression on each
n <- 100
slopes_sim <- rep(NA, 300)
for(s in seq_along(slopes_sim)){
    s_id <- sample(nrow(population_xy), n, replace=FALSE)
    xy_sim <- population_xy[s_id, ]
    colnames(xy_sim) <- c('x_hat', 'y_hat')
    reg_s <- lm(y_hat ~ x_hat, data=xy_sim)
    slopes_sim[s] <- coef(reg_s)[2]
}

# Sampling distribution of the slope
hist(slopes_sim, breaks=25,
    main=NA, border=NA, freq=FALSE,
    xlab=expression(hat(b)[1]))
title('Sample Slopes from the Known Population', font.main=1)
abline(v=population_slope, col=rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty=2)

We check the coverage of both intervals from Simple Regression: the jackknife Normal interval and the bootstrap percentile interval. Inside the resampling loops, we compute each slope as \(\hat{c}_{XY}/\hat{v}_{X}\), which gives the same number as lm but runs faster.

Code 
# Jackknife Normal CI and bootstrap percentile CI for each sample
n_samples <- 300
jack_cis <- matrix(NA, nrow=n_samples, ncol=2)
boot_cis <- matrix(NA, nrow=n_samples, ncol=2)
for(s in seq(n_samples)){
    s_id <- sample(nrow(population_xy), n, replace=FALSE)
    x_sim <- population_xy[s_id, 'x']
    y_sim <- population_xy[s_id, 'y']
    slope_s <- cov(x_sim, y_sim)/var(x_sim)
    # Jackknife SE and Normal CI
    jack_coefs_s <- rep(NA, n)
    for(i in seq_along(jack_coefs_s)){
        x_sim_i <- x_sim[-i]
        y_sim_i <- y_sim[-i]
        jack_coefs_s[i] <- cov(x_sim_i, y_sim_i)/var(x_sim_i)
    }
    jack_se_s <- sqrt((n - 1)/n * sum((jack_coefs_s - mean(jack_coefs_s))^2))
    jack_cis[s, ] <- qnorm(c(.025, .975), slope_s, jack_se_s)
    # Bootstrap percentile CI
    boot_coefs_s <- rep(NA, 399)
    for(b in seq_along(boot_coefs_s)){
        b_id <- sample(n, replace=TRUE)
        x_sim_boot <- x_sim[b_id]
        y_sim_boot <- y_sim[b_id]
        boot_coefs_s[b] <- cov(x_sim_boot, y_sim_boot)/var(x_sim_boot)
    }
    boot_cis[s, ] <- quantile(boot_coefs_s, probs=c(.025, .975))
}

# Percent of intervals that contain the population slope
jack_covered <- population_slope >= jack_cis[, 1] & population_slope <= jack_cis[, 2]
boot_covered <- population_slope >= boot_cis[, 1] & population_slope <= boot_cis[, 2]
c(jackknife_normal=mean(jack_covered), bootstrap_percentile=mean(boot_covered))*100
##     jackknife_normal bootstrap_percentile 
##             95.00000             94.66667

# Plot the first 100 intervals from each method (Red = Missed)
cis_list <- list('Jackknife Normal'=jack_cis[1:100, ], 'Bootstrap Percentile'=boot_cis[1:100, ])
ci_xlim <- range(jack_cis[1:100, ], boot_cis[1:100, ])
par(mfrow=c(1, 2), mar=c(4, 4, 2, 1))
for(method in names(cis_list)){
    cis <- cis_list[[method]]
    covered <- population_slope >= cis[, 1] & population_slope <= cis[, 2]
    plot.new()
    plot.window(xlim=ci_xlim, ylim=c(0, 100))
    segments(cis[, 1], 1:100, cis[, 2], 1:100, lwd=2,
        col=ifelse(covered, rgb(0, 0, 0, .3), rgb(1, 0, 0, .5)))
    abline(v=population_slope, col=rgb(0, 0, 1, .8), lty=2)

Differences in Medians

If we want to test for the differences in medians across groups with independent observations, we can also use notches in the boxplot, as in Multiple Groups. R’s default notches (notch=TRUE) come from a normal-theory approximation. Let each group \(g\) have median \(\tilde{m}_{Y}(g)\), interquartile range \(\hat{\text{IQR}}_{Y}(g)\), observations \(n_{g}\). We can compute standard deviation of the median as \(\tilde{S}_{Y}(g)= \frac{1.25 \hat{\text{IQR}}_{Y}(g)}{1.35 \sqrt{n_{g}}}\). As a rough guess, the interval \(\tilde{m}_{Y}(g) \pm 1.7 \tilde{S}_{Y}(g)\) is the historical default and displayed as a notch in the boxplot (McGill et al. 1978).

The same standard errors give a \(z\)-statistic for the difference in medians, \[\hat{z} = \frac{\tilde{m}_{Y}(1)-\tilde{m}_{Y}(2)}{\sqrt{\tilde{S}_{Y}(1)^2+\tilde{S}_{Y}(2)^2}}.\] Unlike \(\hat{t}\) for means, this ratio has no \(t\)-distribution result, even when the data are normal, so we compare it to the standard normal, which is accurate only in large samples. The standard error of a median depends on how dense the data are near the median, and \(\tilde{S}_{Y}(g)\) fills that in by assuming normality. Specifically, \(\hat{\text{IQR}}_{Y}(g)/1.35\) estimates the standard deviation of normal data, and \(1.25\approx\sqrt{\pi/2}\) is how much more variable a sample median is than a sample mean when the data are normal. Two notches do not overlap when \(|\tilde{m}_{Y}(1)-\tilde{m}_{Y}(2)| > 1.7[\tilde{S}_{Y}(1)+\tilde{S}_{Y}(2)]\). With equal standard errors, that is the same as \(|\hat{z}| > 1.7\sqrt{2} \approx 2.4\), a stricter cutoff than \(1.96\), which is why overlapping notches do not show that the medians are equal.

NoteMust Know

Suppose two groups of \(n_{1}=n_{2}=100\) workers have median wages \(\tilde{m}_{Y}(1)=17\) and \(\tilde{m}_{Y}(2)=15\), and both have an interquartile range of \(7\). Each standard error is \(\tilde{S}_{Y}(g)=1.25\times 7/(1.35\times 10)\approx 0.648\), so \[\hat{z} = \frac{17-15}{\sqrt{0.648^2+0.648^2}} \approx \frac{2}{0.917} \approx 2.18,\] with a \(p\)-value of about \(0.029\). Each notch extends \(1.7\times 0.648\approx 1.10\) from its median, so the notches \([15.90, 18.10]\) and \([13.90, 16.10]\) overlap. So the \(z\)-statistic rejects at the \(5\%\) level even though the notches overlap.

19.2 Bias-Variance Tradeoff

A better fit on the data you have is not automatically better, because a model can hit every point in the sample and still predict new observations poorly.

ImportantKey Definition

Let \(Y^{\mathrm{pred}}(x)\) be a model’s prediction at \(x\) before the sample is drawn, and let \(m_{Y}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}\mid X_{i}=x]\) be the conditional expectation it estimates. The mean squared error of the prediction decomposes into two pieces: \[\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Y^{\mathrm{pred}}(x)-m_{Y}(x)\right)^2\right] = \left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y^{\mathrm{pred}}(x)\right]-m_{Y}(x)\right)^2 + \mathbb{V}\left[Y^{\mathrm{pred}}(x)\right].\] Squared bias, the first piece, is how far the average prediction sits from the truth. Variance, the second piece, is how much the predictions wobble around their own average. The bias-variance tradeoff is that cutting one piece usually raises the other.

The bias-variance tradeoff is useful for thinking about why a tuning parameter matters and how to set it. For local regressions, the bandwidth \(h\) (for LOESS, the span) controls the tradeoff. A large \(h\) lowers variance but raises bias, and a small \(h\) does the opposite. A loess fit narrow enough to interpolate every point has \(\hat{\text{MSE}}=0\) on the fit data, as defined in Model Fit, but generalizes poorly because it has just copied the noise.

  • Wide window (large span, large \(h\)): each local fit uses many observations, so the fitted curve barely changes from sample to sample (low variance). But a nearly flat fit over a wide region cannot follow a relationship that bends, so it is systematically off (high bias).
  • Narrow window (small span, small \(h\)): each local fit uses few observations, so the fitted curve chases noise and swings from sample to sample (high variance). But a narrow window can track curvature closely (low bias).

Schooling takes only a few distinct values, so this section fits local linear LOESS (degree=1), which is more stable than the default local quadratic in small samples.

With span \(4.0\) the fitted curve is nearly a straight line and very stable, and the bootstrap band sits tightly around it. Consistency

The bias-variance tradeoff raises a question: can both be reduced simultaneously as \(n\) grows? For local regression, the answer is yes, provided we shrink the bandwidth as \(n\) grows. The neighborhood shrinks (reducing bias) while the local sample size still grows (reducing variance). This is often written as bandwidth conditions: \[ h_{n} \to 0 \quad\text{and}\quad n h_{n} \to \infty. \] In LOESS language, this corresponds to the span shrinking with \(n\), but not too fast.

NoteMust Know

A common bandwidth rule shrinks \(h\) with the sample size, for example \(h_{n}=n^{-1/5}\). Check the two conditions:

  • \(h_{n}=n^{-1/5}\to 0\): at \(n=100\) it is about \(0.40\); at \(n=10{,}000\) it is about \(0.16\). The neighborhood shrinks.
  • \(n h_{n}=n^{4/5}\to\infty\): at \(n=100\) it is about \(40\); at \(n=10{,}000\) it is about \(1585\). The local sample size still grows.

So bias falls (the window narrows) while variance falls (each local fit uses more data).

Code 
n <- c(100, 10000)
h_n <- n^(-1/5)
rbind(h_n=round(h_n, 3), n_times_h=round(n*h_n, 1))
##             [,1]     [,2]
## h_n        0.398    0.158
## n_times_h 39.800 1584.900

# Simulate n workers with ages evenly spaced from 15 to 70
sim_xy <- function(n){
    x_sim <- seq(15, 70, length.out=n)
    y_sim <- true_m(x_sim) + runif(n, -5E4, 5E4) # true mean plus noise
    return(data.frame(y_sim, x_sim))
}

x_target <- 40  # mid-career age
n_grid <- c(60, 120, 240, 480)
n_rep <- 120

avg_abs_err <- numeric(length(n_grid))
for (ni in seq_along(n_grid)) {
    n <- n_grid[ni]
    span_n <- min(0.9, n^(-1/5)) # shrinks with n
    errs <- replicate(n_rep, {
        xy_sim <- sim_xy(n)
        reg_lo <- loess(y_sim ~ x_sim, data=xy_sim, span=span_n, degree=1)
        y_pred <- predict(reg_lo, newdata=data.frame(x_sim=x_target))
        abs(y_pred - true_m(x_target))
    })
    avg_abs_err[ni] <- mean(errs, na.rm=TRUE)
}

plot(n_grid, avg_abs_err, type='b', pch=16,
    xlab='Sample size (n)',
    ylab='Mean Absolute Error',
    main=NA)

A similar result holds for OLS when the data come from a linear data generating process. Even with unlimited data, however, a misspecified model cannot recover the conditional expectation, as the linear fit to the wage population shows.

Recall

This chapter tested two means with Welch’s \(t\)-test and a regression slope with the theoretical \(t_{n-2}\) distribution. It also used the large-sample \(z\)-statistic, which covers a difference in medians as well. It then decomposed prediction error into squared bias and variance. The USArrests bootstrap showed that the histogram of \(\hat{t}\) roughly matched the theoretical \(t_{n-2}\) density. Treating Wages1 as a known population, the linear fit predicted a mean wage of \(5.96\) at \(12\) years of schooling against a population mean of \(5.67\), a bias that more data does not remove, while the LOESS error was mostly variance that shrank as \(n\) grew.

  1. One benefit of local linear regression is that it is theoretically motivated. Suppose that \(Y_{i}=m_{Y}(X_{i}) + \epsilon_{i}\), where \(m_{Y}(x)\) is the population average of \(Y_{i}\) among observations with \(X_{i}=x\), and \(\epsilon_{i}\) is an unobserved random error that averages zero at each value of \(X_{i}\). For \(X_{i}\) near \(x\), a Taylor approximation gives \[\begin{aligned} m_{Y}(X_{i}) + \epsilon_{i} &\approx m_{Y}(x) + m_{Y}'(x)[X_{i}-x] + \epsilon_{i} \\ &= [m_{Y}(x) - m_{Y}'(x)x ] + m_{Y}'(x)X_{i} + \epsilon_{i} \\ &= b_{0}(x,h) + b_{1}(x,h) X_{i} + \epsilon_{i}. \end{aligned}\] So near \(x\), the population relationship is approximately a line with slope \(b_{1}(x,h)=m_{Y}'(x)\), which is what a local linear regression fits.↩︎
